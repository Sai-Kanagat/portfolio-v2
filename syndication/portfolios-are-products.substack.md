# Portfolios Are Products

Most portfolios are decks pretending to be websites. A hero image, a grid of project thumbnails, a long PDF you have to download to learn anything substantive. The information architecture says *this is a one-way artefact, here to be skimmed.*

I don't think that's enough anymore. Recruiters in 2026 look at hundreds of portfolios. The ones that get remembered behave like products — they have a clear job, reward exploration, have texture, ship.

## The product brief I gave myself

- **Audience:** a hiring manager in Italy with seven minutes between meetings.
- **Job to be done:** decide whether I'm worth a 30-minute first call.
- **Failure mode:** they bounce in 12 seconds because the homepage is generic.
- **Non-goals:** impressing other designers, winning awards, looking trendy.

Every decision hung off those four points.

## What that produced

**Live demos beat case studies.** Every interactive piece — Agora, hand-tracked cursor, colour-physics toy — was a deliberate choice over writing another case-study deck. A recruiter clicks once, has a working thing in their browser, instantly knows I can ship.

**EN/IT toggle as proof, not feature.** Saying "I'm learning Italian" in a CV is cheap. Building a working bilingual site is the same statement, but already shipped.

**The CV is downloadable as ATS-clean PDF, not pretty PDF.** Most designer CVs lose 40% of their content when an ATS parses them. I made mine ugly on purpose — single column, plain typography, every keyword machine-readable.

**Brutal pruning.** I cut three full pages of "process" content because nobody who matters cares about my Figma library structure.

## The lesson

Ask yourself: *if this had to acquire its first 100 users with no advertising, would it?* Most portfolios couldn't. That question is the entire brief.
